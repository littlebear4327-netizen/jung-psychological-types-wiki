# Extraversion

## Definition & Core Mechanism
**Extraversion** is an overarching psychological attitude characterized by a centrifugal movement of [[Libido]] (psychic energy) directed outward toward the external object `(pp. 319, 322, 427)`. In an extraverted attitude:
- The **external object** (people, events, physical reality, societal norms) plays the predominant, decisive role in conscious orientation, decision-making, and judgment `(p. 322)`.
- Psychic interest, energy, and values flow continuously from the subject toward the objective environment `(pp. 322–323)`.
- The subject actively adapts to and is assimilated by the conditions of the outer world `(pp. 323–325)`.

## Conscious Manifestation & Adaptation
- **Objective Determinism**: The extravert’s subjective inner life is subordinated to external requirements. Actions, morality, and goals are derived from the expectations and consensus of the milieu `(pp. 322–324)`.
- **Adaptation vs Conformism**: When balanced, extraversion produces genuine social adaptation, community leadership, and practical effectiveness; when exaggerated, it collapses into hollow conformism, superficial trend-following, and loss of the authentic self `(pp. 324–325)`.
- **Bodily and Psychic Neglect**: In extreme cases, the extravert becomes so thoroughly identified with the object that they completely ignore their own physical health, emotional needs, and subjective integrity `(pp. 324–325)`.

## Unconscious Compensation & Pathology
- **The Compensatory Law**: The more one-sidedly conscious extraversion focuses on the outer object, the more the unconscious develops an infantile, archaic, and egocentric counterposition `(pp. 326–328)`.
- **The Subjective Shadow**: The repressed subjective factor takes on an archaic, infantile, and ruthlessly selfish character. The extravert who claims to act purely for others' welfare is secretly driven by archaic power-wishes and narcissistic demands `(pp. 327–328)`.
- **Characteristic Neurosis**: **Hysteria** is the quintessential neurosis of the extravert `(p. 325)`. Unconscious conflicts express themselves somatically through physical conversion symptoms, dramatic poses, and emotional outbursts designed to manipulate the object `(pp. 325–326)`.
