# Libido (Psychic Energy)

## Definition & Foundational Premise
**Libido**—defined by C. G. Jung in conscious departure from Sigmund Freud's pan-sexual formulation—denotes generalized **Psychic Energy** (*Psychische Energie*), representing the dynamic intensity and quantitative value of psychic processes `(pp. 433–434)`:
- "By libido I understand psychic energy. Psychic energy is the intensity of a psychic process, its psychological value" `(p. 433)`.
- Libido is an energic, quantitative concept, distinct from qualitative descriptive contents:
  $$ \text{Psychic Value} \propto \text{Quantity of Libido Invested} $$
- Jung insists that psychic energy cannot be measured in concrete ergs or calories, but can be evaluated empirically through comparative psychological values—namely, the relative power of attraction, determination, and persistence exerted by specific ideas, complexes, or attitudes `(pp. 433–434)`.

## Energetic Principles Governing the Psyche
Jung applies the core laws of physical energetics to psychic dynamics `(pp. 202, 407, 433)`:

1. **The Principle of Equivalence (Conservation of Energy)**:
   - Energy is never destroyed in the psychic apparatus; if libido disappears from a conscious idea, function, or institution (through loss of interest or repression), it inevitably reappears in an equivalent quantity elsewhere—either transferred to a new conscious interest, converted into somatic symptoms, or sinking into the unconscious to energize autonomous complexes `(pp. 401, 433)`.
2. **The Principle of Entropy (Degradation of Potential)**:
   - Libido flows naturally from areas of higher potential to areas of lower potential, striving toward psychic equilibrium. Total balance would equal psychic inertia; hence, life requires continual renewal of tensions `(pp. 202, 433)`.
3. **The Principle of Polarity (Opposites as Energy Source)**:
   - Psychic energy can only be generated through the tension between pairs of opposites (e.g., conscious vs. unconscious, extraversion vs. introversion, rational vs. irrational):
     - "There can be no energy without pairs of opposites... energy necessarily presupposes a prior opposition, a difference of potential" `(pp. 202, 407)`.

## Dynamics: Progression vs. Regression
Jung identifies two fundamental directions of libidinous movement `(pp. 433–434)`:

### 1. Progression (*Progression*)
- The continuous, forward-moving adaptation of psychic energy to environmental demands and conscious goals `(p. 433)`.
- It occurs when the conscious attitude and functions are sufficiently flexible to meet the challenges presented by internal and external reality `(pp. 416, 433)`.

### 2. Regression (*Regression*)
- The backward movement of libido occurring when progression is blocked by an insurmountable external obstacle, a failure of conscious attitude, or an unresolved internal conflict `(pp. 433–434)`.
- **Not Merely Pathological**: Unlike Freud, who viewed regression almost exclusively as an infantile fixation, Jung emphasizes its teleological necessity:
  - Regression withdraws energy from the barren conscious surface and infuses the unconscious matrix, reactivating primordial images and forgotten instinctual reserves `(pp. 118, 434)`.
  - It prepares the psychic ground for the birth of the `[[concepts/Symbol|uniting symbol]]` and the activation of the `[[concepts/Transcendent-Function|transcendent function]]` `(pp. 120–122, 455–456)`.

## Libido in the Typological Spectrum
- **Extraversion**: The centrifugal, outward movement of libido toward external objects `(pp. 323–325, 407)`.
- **Introversion**: The centripetal, inward movement of libido toward the subject and the subjective factor `(pp. 360–363, 430)`.
- **Attitude Shifts**: The conscious allocation of libido dictates which psychological function (`[[concepts/Rational-vs-Irrational-Functions|Thinking, Feeling, Sensation, Intuition]]`) becomes differentiated and superior `(pp. 405, 416)`.
