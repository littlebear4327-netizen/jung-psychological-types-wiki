# The Self

## Definition & Foundational Premise
The **Self** (*Das Selbst*) is defined by C. G. Jung as the subject of the total psyche, encompassing both the conscious mind and the unconscious domains `(pp. 437–438)`:
- "By the self I understand the totality of the psyche. The conscious mind is not the whole of it; the unconscious is also a part" `(p. 437)`.
- In Jungian structural topography, the **Ego** is merely the central complex and subject of the conscious field `(p. 406)`, whereas the **Self** is the center and circumference of the total psychic system:
  $$ \text{Self} = \text{Ego (Consciousness)} \cup \text{Personal Unconscious} \cup \text{Collective Unconscious} $$
- Jung notes the asymmetric hierarchical relationship between the two:
  - "The ego is only the subject of my consciousness, while the self is the subject of my total psyche, which also includes the unconscious" `(p. 437)`.
  - The Ego is related to the Self as the moved is to the mover, or as an object to a subject `(p. 437)`.

## Epistemological Status: Empirical Postulate & Transcendental Concept
Jung strictly delineates the epistemological boundaries of the Self to prevent metaphysical conflation `(pp. 437–438)`:
1. **Empirical Postulate**: Insofar as the unconscious cannot be directly inspected or quantitatively measured, the total psyche cannot be fully circumscribed by conscious intellection. The Self is therefore an indispensable working hypothesis, an empirical postulate describing an observed psychic ordering principle `(p. 437)`.
2. **Transcendental Concept**: Because its totality transcends the cognitive reach of the ego-consciousness, the Self cannot be formulated logically in definitive propositional terms; it can only be expressed symbolically `(pp. 437–438)`.

## Symbolic Manifestations of the Self
When the unconscious produces archetypal images representing psychic equilibrium, integration, or totality, these images represent the Self `(pp. 437–438)`:
- **Geometric Symbols**: Mandalas, circles, squared circles, crosses, and quaternities `(pp. 437–438)`.
- **Anthropos & Divine Figures**: The cosmic man, the inner Christ, the Buddha, the philosopher's stone (*lapis philosophorum*), or the divine child `(pp. 438, 452)`.
- **Unifying Function**: The emergence of Self-symbols marks the successful resolution of conscious/unconscious polarities through the `[[concepts/Transcendent-Function|transcendent function]]` and the birth of the `[[concepts/Symbol|uniting symbol]]` `(pp. 121, 455–456)`.

## The Self in the Context of Typology & Individuation
- **Goal of Individuation**: `[[concepts/Individuation]]` consists in the conscious differentiation and subsequent reconciliation of all four cognitive functions (`[[concepts/Rational-vs-Irrational-Functions|Thinking, Feeling, Sensation, Intuition]]`) and the two attitudes (`[[concepts/Extraversion|Extraversion]]` and `[[concepts/Introversion|Introversion]]`). This multi-dimensional integration converges on the Self as the archetype of wholeness `(pp. 426–428, 437)`.
- **Ego-Inflation vs. Integration**: If the ego confuses itself with the Self (ego-inflation), pathologically usurping the autonomy of the unconscious, psychic equilibrium is shattered, leading to neurosis, psychosis, or violent compensatory `[[concepts/Enantiodromia|enantiodromia]]` `(pp. 406–407, 437)`.
