# Symbol (The Living Symbol)

## Definition & Foundational Premise
The **Symbol** (*Symbol*) is defined by C. G. Jung as the best possible formulation of a relatively unknown, transcendent psychic reality that cannot be more clearly or directly expressed by discursive intellect `(pp. 449–456)`:
- "The symbol always presupposes that the chosen expression is the best possible description, or formula, of a relatively unknown fact; a fact, however, which is none the less recognized or postulated as existing" `(p. 449)`.
- A genuine symbol is not a deliberate intellectual fabrication or code; it is an organic, autonomous creation of the total psyche, originating in the unconscious and clothing an archetypal intuition in concrete empirical form `(pp. 450–451)`.

## Critical Distinction: Symbol vs. Sign (Semiotic vs. Symbolic)
Jung draws a sharp epistemological line between a **semiotic sign** (*Zeichen*) and a **genuine symbol** (*Symbol*) `(pp. 449–451)`:

| Dimension | Semiotic Sign (*Zeichen*) | Genuine Symbol (*Symbol*) | Page Ref |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Referent** | Refers to a known, identifiable thing or conventional concept. | Refers to a relatively unknown, unconscious, or transcendent reality. | pp. 449–450 |
| **Epistemic Function** | Abbreviation, label, or token (e.g., a badge, algebraic symbol, or trade logo). | Epistemic bridge; pregnant with limitless intuitive meaning. | pp. 449–450 |
| **Lifespan / Status** | Static and permanent; meaning remains fixed by social consensus. | Dynamic and organic; either **Living** or **Dead**. | pp. 450–451 |
| **Psychological Stance** | Reductive: traces contents backward to known causes (Freud/Adler). | Synthetic / Constructive: forward-looking (*teleological*), indicating future integration. | pp. 403–405, 451 |

## The Concept of the "Living Symbol" (*Lebendiges Symbol*)
Jung explains that a symbol is not an objective property of an object, but a psychological attitude of the perceiving consciousness `(pp. 450–452)`:
1. **The Living Symbol**: A symbol is alive only so long as it is pregnant with inexhaustible meaning, evoking numinous emotion, wonder, and transformative psychic movement `(p. 450)`.
2. **The Death of the Symbol**: As soon as conscious intellect succeeds in giving birth to its meaning—translating the mystery completely into clear, rational concepts—the symbol dies. It degenerates into a historical fossil, a dogmatic emblem, or a dead sign `(p. 451)`.

## The "Uniting Symbol" (*Vereinigendes Symbol*)
The most profound psychological category of symbol in Jung's system is the **Uniting Symbol** `(pp. 451–456)`:
- When consciousness is paralyzed by the war of opposites (e.g., thinking vs. feeling, conscious duty vs. unconscious desire, ego vs. shadow), neither side can overpower the other without psychic mutilation `(pp. 116–118)`.
- The accumulating `[[concepts/Libido|libido]]` regresses into the collective unconscious, activating the `[[concepts/Transcendent-Function|transcendent function]]`. Out of this unconscious fermentation, the psyche produces a paradoxical, synthetic image that unites both sides into a higher equilibrium `(pp. 120–122, 455–456)`.
- **Examples of Uniting Symbols**:
  - The Holy Grail, the Philosopher's Stone (*lapis*), the Divine Child, the Mandala, the Tree of Life, Christ, or the reconciled opposites of Lion and Unicorn `(pp. 437–438, 452–454)`.

## Epistemological Stance: *Esse in Anima* (Being in the Soul)
- In defining the ontological status of the symbol, Jung asserts the primacy of psychic reality: *esse in anima* `(pp. 64–66, 454)`.
- A symbol is neither purely physical (material object) nor purely metaphysical (abstract idea). It exists in the living medium of the psyche (*anima*), possessing psychological reality and empirical efficacy equal to or exceeding physical objects `(pp. 65, 454)`.

## Role in the Individuation Journey
- In `[[concepts/Individuation]]`, symbols serve as milestones and catalysts `(pp. 426–428)`.
- They rescue the psyche from the sterile pendulum of `[[concepts/Enantiodromia|enantiodromia]]` by elevating consciousness to a broader integration, culminating in symbols of the `[[concepts/Self]]` (mandalas and quaternities) which embody total wholeness `(pp. 437–438, 456)`.
